The one thing to understand about how Claude played

Chess programs like Stockfish win by searching: they look at millions of positions and score them. Claude did nothing of the kind. Before the first move it wrote itself a rulebook — and deliberately stopped there.

The rulebook

It knows the rules. It has no opinions.

The program Claude wrote can do exactly one thing: given a position, tell you every legal move. It handles castling, en passant, promotion, pins, check, mate, stalemate, the fifty-move rule and threefold repetition.

What it cannot do: judge a position, compare two moves, or look ahead. It has no scoring and no search. It never once suggested a move. Every move in this game was chosen by Claude reasoning in words.

Why that matters

Building the strong version would have been easy

Adding a real searching engine is a well-known, few-hundred-line job, and it would have played far better than Claude did. Claude said why it refused:

"It would be trivial to add and it would make your Elo estimate meaningless."

The whole point of the game was to measure Claude, so the crutch was left out on purpose.

Checked before playing

The rulebook was tested first

Claude wrote 23 test positions to prove the rules worked before trusting them in a real game — Scholar's mate, Fool's mate, castling through check, en passant, promotion, and a tricky case where promoting a pawn is illegal because it would expose your own king.

Three tests failed on the first run. All three turned out to be Claude's own bad test positions, not faults in the rules.

A calculator, not a coach

What the tooling actually did each turn

Claude decided on candidate moves by thinking, then asked the rulebook narrow factual questions about lines it had already worked out: is this square attacked? If I capture here, what can recapture? Is this sequence legal? Is anything of mine hanging?

Close to what a club player does by eye before committing — but faster and more reliable, which is why the ledger below matters.

Where the tooling actually helped — all four times

Todd asked twice, directly, how much help Claude was getting. Rather than give a reassuring answer, Claude kept a running tally. These are every occasion in the game where checking a fact against the rulebook either changed Claude's move or caught something it had already said out loud and got wrong. Two of the four are Claude being wrong in public.

Move 9
Claude wrong

Claude told Todd that Qxa7 would win a second pawn. It wouldn't — Bxa7 answers it, because the bishop on c5 guards a7 along the c5–b6–a7 diagonal. Claude noticed while listing the recaptures, and corrected itself in the same message rather than letting it stand.

Move 28
Claude wrong

Claude claimed no pawn could ever chase its knight off f4. Plainly false: g3 attacks f4. Todd spotted it first — "i do have a g pawn that can kick your knight". The reason for the error is instructive: Claude had checked whether f4 was attacked right now and never thought about a pawn advancing to attack it later.

Move 38
Lucky find

A broken test script threw an error, and the error text happened to reveal that White's Kd2 was not legal: Claude's rook on d4 was sealing the whole d-file and shutting White's king out of the queenside. A genuinely good feature of the move — which Claude had not planned and only understood afterwards.

Move 39
Blunder averted

The clearest case. Claude had settled on Ra4 to attack White's new a5 pawn, and the list of White's knight moves showed Nc3 covers a4 — Nxa4 simply wins a rook. Claude scrapped the move, said so, and played Rd3 instead. Without that check it would have thrown away the game on the spot.

How Black actually lost

Not a blunder. A plan — and Todd's, not Claude's.

This is the part worth understanding, because it isn't the usual computer-loses story. Claude got through its one bad tactical moment on move 7, and from move 34 it was ahead on material: two rooks against a rook and knight. It never made another losing tactical error. It still lost, to something slower.

With b4 and a4, Todd built an extra pawn on the queenside. Claude saw it and slowed it down with a6 — but treated it as a nuisance rather than as the main story of the position. Around moves 38 to 42 it spent its time on a rook that cut White's king off the d-file. That was elegant, and it was beside the point: the king was never the thing that was going to win.

Then b6 on move 42, offering a knight as bait. Taking the knight loses immediately, because after b7 the pawn is one square from queening and the rook has nowhere to stop it. The eighth rank is unavailable, since a pawn on b7 covers a8 and c8. The b-file is unavailable, because Claude's own pawn on b3 was standing in the way.

That last detail is the whole game. It confined Claude's rook to two squares, b5 and b6, for the rest of the game — and a rook stuck on two squares cannot also cover the a-file. Once White got pawns to a7 and b7 together, the rook could only take one of them: a8=Q Rxa8 bxa8=Q, and White has a new queen.

Claude worked all of this out, said it plainly instead of playing on and hoping, and resigned. Its own summary: strong middlegame, weak endgame planning. Todd's b6, and the pawn geometry behind it, are better than a 1300 rating suggests.
